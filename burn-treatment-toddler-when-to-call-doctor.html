Child Poisoning Prevention at Home: Essential Safety Steps

Poisoning is one of the most common injuries in young children, with around 3 million people—mostly under age 5—swallowing or coming into contact with a poisonous substance each year . The good news is that childhood poisoning is preventable. Because most poisonings happen at home, often when a parent is nearby but distracted, taking simple precautions can protect your child . Here is what you need to know.

Understanding the Risks

A poison is any substance harmful to the body if swallowed, inhaled, injected, or absorbed through the skin . The top dangers for children include:

Medicines (prescription and over-the-counter)

Cleaning and laundry products (including detergent packets)

Liquid nicotine for e-cigarettes and vapes

Antifreeze and windshield washer fluid

Certain pesticides and furniture polish

Hydrocarbon fuels (gasoline, kerosene, lamp oil)

Cosmetics, nail polish, and personal care products

Button-cell batteries (found in remote controls, greeting cards, and toys) Lead poisoning is another serious risk, especially in homes built before 1978. Children under 6 are at greatest risk from lead paint dust, contaminated soil, or drinking water from lead pipes .

10 Essential Steps to Poison-Proof Your Home

Store products safely and out of sight. Keep medicines, cleaning products, pesticides, and other chemicals in their original containers with child-resistant caps. Store them in locked cabinets that are high and out of sight, not just out of reach .

Use safety latches on accessible cabinets, especially under sinks. Remember that safety caps and latches are "child-resistant," not "childproof" .

Check labels every time you give medicine. Use the dosing device that came with the medicine (never a kitchen spoon) . Turn on a light to avoid mistakes, especially at night .

Never call medicine "candy" or take it in front of children—they imitate adults .

Keep products in original containers. Never put poisonous products into food or drink containers (even if relabeled), as children may not be able to read .

Dispose of unused medicines properly. Check if they can be flushed, or mix them with coffee grounds or kitty litter, seal in a bag, and throw away after concealing personal info. Use community take-back programs if available .

Be extra vigilant during visits to grandparents' or friends' homes, or when you have visitors. Changes in routine and unfamiliar places often have lower safety barriers .

Secure button batteries in remote controls, key fobs, and musical cards. If swallowed, seek immediate emergency care—tissue damage can occur in 2 hours .

Know your plants and remove poisonous ones from your home and yard .

Post the Poison Control number (1) where you can see it or save it in your phone. The webPOISONCONTROL tool is also available online .

Lead Poisoning Prevention

For homes built before 1978, be aware of lead paint that is chipping or deteriorating. Keep children away from peeling paint and bare soil, and wash their hands and toys frequently. Talk to your pediatrician about a blood lead test if you suspect exposure .
