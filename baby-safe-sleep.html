Baby Safe Sleep: A Parent's Guide to Reducing the Risk of SIDS

Going home with a new baby is exciting, but it can be scary, too (Source: NIH). One of the most important things you can do to keep your baby safe is to follow the guidelines for safe sleep. This guide provides the evidence-based information you need to create a safe sleep environment for your baby, reducing the risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) and other sleep-related injuries.

What is SIDS?

Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) is the sudden, unexplained death of an infant younger than one year old . It is sometimes called "crib death" because many babies who die of SIDS are found in their cribs . SIDS is the leading cause of death in children between one month and one year old, with most deaths occurring when babies are between one month and four months old .

The ABCs of Safe Sleep

The safest way for a baby to sleep is by following the "ABCs": Alone, on their Back, in a Crib. This simple framework is the foundation of every safe sleep guideline.

1. Alone

Your baby should sleep alone, in their own sleep space. This means no bed-sharing with parents, siblings, or pets. Room-sharing, however, is highly recommended.

2. On their Back

Place your baby on their back for every sleep, even for short naps (Source: NIH). This position is the single most effective way to reduce the risk of SIDS. "Tummy time" is for when babies are awake and someone is watching (Source: NIH).

3. In a Crib

Your baby should sleep in a crib, bassinet, or play yard that meets current safety standards.

Creating a Safe Sleep Environment

The Sleep Surface

Firm and Flat: Use a firm sleep surface, such as a crib mattress covered with a fitted sheet (Source: NIH).

No Soft Bedding: Keep soft objects and loose bedding away from your baby's sleep area (Source: NIH). This includes pillows, blankets, bumper pads, and stuffed animals.

Room-Sharing

Having your baby sleep in your room for at least the first six months is strongly recommended (Source: NIH). Your baby should sleep close to you, but on a separate surface designed for infants, such as a crib or bassinet (Source: NIH). Room-sharing can reduce the risk of SIDS by as much as 50%.

Temperature

Make sure that your baby doesn't get too hot (Source: NIH). Keep the room at a comfortable temperature for an adult. Dress your baby in light sleep clothing and avoid overheating.

Avoid Smoke Exposure

Do not smoke during pregnancy or allow anyone to smoke near your baby (Source: NIH). Exposure to smoke significantly increases the risk of SIDS.

Safe Sleep Devices

Pacifiers

Offering a pacifier at naptime and bedtime can help reduce the risk of SIDS. If you are breastfeeding, wait until breastfeeding is well established before introducing a pacifier.

Swaddling

Swaddling can be a useful technique for calming a baby, but it should be stopped as soon as a baby shows signs of rolling over.

What to Avoid

Car Seats and Strollers: Avoid letting your baby sleep in car seats, strollers, or other seating devices for extended periods, especially at home.

Adult Beds: Never put your baby to sleep on an adult bed, couch, or armchair.

Positioners and Wedges: Do not use sleep positioners or wedges to prop your baby on their side.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I call the doctor about my baby's sleep?

If you have any concerns about your baby's sleep patterns or behavior, or if your baby is having difficulty breathing, appears unwell, or is difficult to wake, call your doctor immediately.

How long does it take for a baby to get used to sleeping on their back?

Most babies will adapt to sleeping on their backs within a few days, but it may take a week or two. Consistency is key; always place your baby on their back for every sleep.

What home remedies can help a baby sleep safely?

There are no "home remedies" for safe sleep. The only safe practices are those recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics: back to sleep, a firm sleep surface, and a clear crib.

Is it safe to use a baby monitor?

Yes, baby monitors can provide peace of mind, but they are not a substitute for safe sleep practices. A monitor should not be used to justify unsafe sleep environments, such as bed-sharing.

Can I use a sleep positioner to prevent flat head?

No, sleep positioners and wedges are not safe and have been linked to infant deaths. To prevent flat spots, use "tummy time" when your baby is awake and supervised.

How long should I room-share with my baby?

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends room-sharing for at least the first six months and ideally for the first full year.

What should I do if my baby rolls onto their stomach?

Continue to place your baby on their back to sleep. Once they can roll over both ways on their own, you don't need to reposition them. However, ensure the sleep area is clear of soft objects and loose bedding.
