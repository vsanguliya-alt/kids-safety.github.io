Burn Treatment for Children in Houston: What Parents Need to Know

A burn injury is a serious medical event, especially for a child. In Houston, parents have access to specialized pediatric burn care, but the first and most critical steps happen at home or on the scene. Knowing how to respond quickly and correctly can make a significant difference in your child's recovery.

Immediate First Aid for a Child's Burn

If your child sustains a burn, the priority is to stop the burning process and prevent further tissue damage .

Remove the child from the heat source .

Cool the burn immediately. Run cool (not cold) water over the burn for several minutes. Do not use ice, as this can cause additional damage to the injured skin .

Do not apply ointments, powders, oils, or sprays, including butter or grease, to the burned area. These "home remedies" can worsen the injury and increase the risk of infection .

Protect the burn by covering it loosely with a dry, sterile gauze bandage or a clean cloth .

If clothing is stuck to the burn, do not pull it off. Instead, cut the fabric around the burn and leave the stuck portion in place .

Do not break any blisters that have formed, as this can increase the risk of infection .

Red Flags: When to Seek Emergency Care in Houston

For children, certain burns require immediate medical attention, either at the ER or a specialized burn center. You should call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room if the burn involves :

The face, hands, feet, eyes, mouth, genitals, or a major joint.

A large area of the body, generally larger than the child's hand or about 3 inches.

A deep burn that appears white, brown, black, leathery, or charred.

A burn that goes all the way around an arm or leg.

A burn caused by electricity or chemicals.

Signs of smoke inhalation: trouble breathing, hoarseness, cough, confusion, or soot around the mouth or nose .

In all cases, even if a burn appears minor, it is advisable to contact your child's healthcare provider. They can assess if a tetanus shot is needed, as burns can be tetanus-prone wounds . For pain, a dose of acetaminophen or ibuprofen appropriate for your child's weight can be given.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I call a doctor about treatment?

If your child has difficulty breathing, persistent high fever, lethargy, or symptoms lasting beyond expected, contact a healthcare provider.

Is treatment normal at this age?

Many variations are normal. Red-flag signs (listed above) mean you should seek care.

What helps at home?

Hydration, rest, and age-appropriate comfort measures. Always follow NHS/AAP guidance for medications.

How long does it last?

Most childhood episodes resolve within a few days. Persistent or worsening symptoms need evaluation.
