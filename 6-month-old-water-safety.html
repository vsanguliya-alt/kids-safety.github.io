6-Month-Old Water Safety: Red Flags vs Normal

At 6 months old, a baby reaches a major milestone: they can start drinking small amounts of water. This change shifts water safety from a strict "avoid" to a new set of rules about how much water is safe, how to give it, and how to prevent drowning hazards that are now more relevant than ever.

What Is Normal: Introducing Water Safely

For the first 6 months, breast milk or formula provides all the hydration a baby needs. However, around 6 months, as solid foods are introduced, you can begin offering your baby water .

Normal practices at 6 months:

Offer small amounts: Start with just a few sips of water at a time. This helps babies practice drinking from a cup and can prevent constipation as they start solids .

Boil and cool water: Until your baby is 12 months old, the water you give them to drink should be boiled and then cooled to a safe temperature to ensure it's free from harmful bacteria .

Use a cup: Offer water in a sippy cup or an open cup, not a bottle, to help them learn this new skill.

Continue milk feeds: Breast milk or formula should still be the primary source of nutrition and hydration .

Red Flags: When to Worry

1. Water Intoxication

While rare, giving a baby too much water can lead to water intoxication (hyponatremia). This condition occurs when excess water dilutes the sodium in the baby's bloodstream, which is especially dangerous for their immature kidneys .

Signs of water intoxication include:

Irritability

Drowsiness or lethargy

Low body temperature

Puffiness of the face

If you notice these signs, especially after a baby has consumed a significant amount of water, seek emergency care immediately .

2. Drowning Risks

Drowning is a leading cause of injury in infants, and it can happen silently and quickly . A baby can drown in as little as 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) of water .

Key drowning red flags and precautions:

Never leave a baby alone in the bath. Most infant drownings happen in the bathtub during a lapse in supervision . If you need to leave the room, even for a moment, take your baby with you .

Empty all water containers immediately. Buckets, wading pools, and coolers with melted ice can become deadly hazards .

Keep toilet lids closed. Babies can topple headfirst into the toilet bowl and be unable to get out. Use safety latches for extra protection .

Secure swimming pools. Any pool, including large inflatable ones, must be surrounded by a four-sided fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate .

Supervision is non-negotiable. A responsible adult should always be within an arm's length of a baby near any water, a practice known as "touch supervision" . Do not rely on bath seats or flotation devices for safety, as they are not a substitute for your attention and can tip over .

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my 6-month-old's water intake?

Call your pediatrician if you notice signs of water intoxication, such as irritability, drowsiness, or puffiness of the face . Also, seek emergency care if your child has a near-drowning experience or you have any serious concerns.

Q: How much water can a 6-month-old have?

Start with just a few sips at a time, particularly during mealtimes. It is about practice, not hydration at this stage .

Q: Does my 6-month-old need water in hot weather?

No. Your baby should continue to get all the hydration they need from breast milk or formula, even in hot weather. Extra water can fill their tiny stomach and displace essential nutrients .

Q: Is tap water safe for my 6-month-old?

Town tap water needs to be boiled and cooled until your baby is 12 months old . Avoid mineral water due to its high salt and mineral content, which is hard on a baby's kidneys .

Q: Can my 6-month-old drown in a bathtub?

Yes. Babies can drown in very shallow water. Never leave your baby unattended in the bath, even for a second .

Q: When should I go to the ER for a water safety issue?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has a near-drowning incident, shows signs of water intoxication (irritability, drowsiness), or has had a seizure .
