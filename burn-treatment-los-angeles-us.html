Burn Treatment for Children in Los Angeles: What Parents Need to Know

Burn injuries in children are a serious concern, and Los Angeles parents have access to some of the nation's leading specialized pediatric burn care resources. Knowing how to respond with proper first aid and understanding when to seek emergency care is critical for protecting your child.

Step 1: Immediate First Aid for a Burn

If your child sustains a burn, take these steps immediately to minimize damage and prevent infection:

Remove the child from the heat source and cool the area by running cool (not cold) water over the burn for about 5 minutes. Do not use ice, as it can cause further tissue damage.

Do not apply ointments, powders, oils, or sprays—including butter or grease—to the burned area, as these "home remedies" can worsen the injury and increase infection risk.

Protect the burn by covering it loosely with a dry, sterile gauze bandage or a clean cloth. If clothing is stuck to the burn, do not pull it off; instead, cut the fabric around the burn and leave the stuck portion in place.

Do not break any blisters, as an intact blister acts as a natural barrier against infection.

Step 2: When to Seek Emergency Care in Los Angeles

For children, certain burns require immediate medical attention at an emergency room or specialized burn center. Call 911 or go to the nearest ER if the burn has any of the following characteristics:

Location: The burn is on the face, head, hands, feet, eyes, mouth, genitals, or over a major joint.

Size: The burn covers a large area.

Appearance: The burn has big blisters, or appears white, waxy, leathery, brown, or charred (deep burn).

Circumferential: The burn goes all the way around an arm or leg.

Cause: The burn was caused by fire, electricity, or chemicals.

Associated symptoms: Your child is having difficulty breathing, is coughing, or was involved in a serious accident.

Even if a burn appears minor, it is advisable to contact your child's healthcare provider. They can assess if a tetanus shot is needed, as burns can be tetanus-prone wounds. For pain, a weight-appropriate dose of acetaminophen or ibuprofen can be given if your child has no conditions preventing their use.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for a minor burn?

Call your pediatrician for any burn that is not a medical emergency. Even for a small burn, professional advice can ensure proper healing and help you determine if a tetanus shot is needed.

Q: How long should I cool a burn with water?

Run cool water over the burn for about 5 minutes. Do not use ice, as this can cause further damage.

Q: What home remedies can I use for a child's burn?

Do not use any home remedies. Avoid butter, oils, toothpaste, or any ointments unless prescribed by a doctor, as these can worsen the burn and lead to infection.

Q: Should I break a blister on a burn?

No. Do not break any blisters. An intact blister acts as a natural barrier against infection.

Q: When should I go to the ER for a burn?

Go to the ER or call 911 immediately if the burn is on the face, hands, feet, eyes, or groin; covers a large area; is deep (white/charred); or if your child has trouble breathing or was involved in a fire or serious accident.

Q: Does my child need a tetanus shot after a burn?

Ask your child's provider if a tetanus shot is necessary. Burns can be a pathway for tetanus, especially if the wound is dirty or deep.
